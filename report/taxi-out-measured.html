Taxi-Out, Measured

Most of the error in predicting aircraft ground delay sits in a handful of flights, and the largest share of those are not slow aircraft. They are clocks that were never read.

How long an aircraft spends between leaving its gate and getting airborne, why a squared error metric decides what the problem actually is, and what the measurements say.

01 · the thing being predicted

A plane leaves its gate. Nothing happens for a while.

When an aircraft is ready to depart, a tug pushes it back from the gate. It then has to reach the runway and take off. The clock running between those two moments is called taxi-out time, and at a busy European hub it averages about seventeen minutes.

Very little of that is driving. The distance from stand to runway is typically a couple of kilometres at walking-to-cycling pace, five or six minutes of actual movement. The rest is waiting: for a tug, for a path across the apron, and above all in a queue at the runway threshold, where aircraft depart one at a time and each departure must be spaced from the one before it.

02 · why anyone pays for this

An engine burning fuel while going nowhere

A twin-engine narrowbody burns something on the order of ten kilograms of fuel a minute while taxiing. Multiply by the minutes spent stationary in a queue, then by the couple of thousand departures a day at a large hub, and ground delay becomes a measurable share of an airport's total emissions, burned without moving a single passenger closer to anywhere.

Predicting taxi-out accurately does two things. It lets an airport identify when it is operating beyond comfortable capacity, and it lets a controller hold an aircraft at the gate with its engines off instead of in a queue with them running. The second is the one that saves the fuel.

This is the subject of the 2026 PRC Data Challenge, run by EUROCONTROL's Performance Review Commission with the OpenSky Network.

03 · the scoring rule, which is not a detail

What "most accurate" turns out to mean

Entries are ranked by root mean square error. For each flight you take the difference between your prediction and the truth, square it, average across all flights, and take the square root. The squaring is the whole story, and it is worth slowing down on, because it quietly decides what the entire project should be about.

Squaring means a prediction that misses by ten minutes is not ten times worse than one that misses by one minute. It is a hundred times worse. A metric built this way barely notices a large number of small errors and is dominated by a small number of large ones.

A metric that squares its errors is not asking you to be right on average. It is asking you not to be badly wrong.

Under this metric the useful question is not how well a model predicts taxi-out in general. It is which flights it is badly wrong about, and why.

04 · a trap in the data

The target is arithmetic, not a measurement

Each departure in the dataset carries several timestamps. The two that matter here are the moment the aircraft came off its stand, and the moment it took off. Taxi-out is not measured separately. It is the difference between them. Across all departure rows in the 2025 data, takeoff minus off-block equals the published taxi-out exactly: maximum discrepancy zero.

That creates a problem for whoever builds the competition. If both timestamps were present in the scored set, subtracting one from the other would score a perfect zero and demonstrate nothing. One of them has to be removed, and which one defines the entire task.

The direction of the task

The takeoff time is present and the off-block time is gone, so the prediction runs backwards from the intuitive direction: you know when the aircraft got airborne, and you work out when it must have left the gate.

This is not deducible from the training data, where both timestamps are present. It is a property of the scored file, established by reading that file's null counts.

05 · the first model

A respectable number that explains nothing

With the leak understood, the rest is ordinary work. Build features that are available at prediction time (the airport, the stand, the runway, the aircraft type, the hour of day, how busy the airfield was, a per-stand estimate of how long the drive takes when nothing is in the way) and fit a gradient-boosted tree.

That is a real improvement over guessing, and it is the point where tuning usually begins: more features, deeper trees, an ensemble. None of that is worth doing before the number is decomposed.

06 · opening up the error

Almost all of it is in almost none of the rows

Sort every flight in the validation set by how badly it was predicted, worst first, then walk along that list adding up the squared errors. If the model's mistakes were spread evenly, the line would climb the diagonal. It does not.

So the question is what those flights are.

07 · the first answer, which is not about aviation

The flights with no paperwork

Each movement recorded by an airport is matched to a flight record from the European network manager, the system that holds the callsign, the operator, the aircraft type, and the network's own off-block time. For about fifteen departures in a thousand, that match fails. Not partially: when it fails, every field from that record is missing at once. One join failing, not four independent gaps.

These are the expensive rows.

The row worth staring at is the median. A typical unmatched flight taxis for ; a typical matched one for . About sixteen minutes each. In their ordinary behaviour the two groups are indistinguishable.

What differs is how far their bad days go. The 99th percentile is more than four times higher, and of the departures in 2025 that took more than six hours belong to this small group.

The hardest part of this aviation problem is a database join that sometimes does not fire.

A missing record is not a property of the flight. It is a property of the recording. That raises the obvious suspicion about the long tail: that it is a property of the recording too.

08 · the second answer

One airport writes the schedule where the pushback should be

Split the unmatched flights by airport and one dominates. Rome Fiumicino (LIRF) has of them in 2025, under a fifteenth of the group, and those flights carry of the group's spread.

The reason is in the timestamps. For of LIRF's unmatched departures, the recorded off-block time is identical to the scheduled departure time, to the second. At every other airport, the same coincidence happens to of unmatched flights. The aircraft did not push back at exactly its scheduled minute. The schedule was written into the field where the pushback should have been.

For those rows, the published taxi-out is simply takeoff minus scheduled departure. When a flight left four hours late, its "taxi-out" is four hours. None of that is an aircraft waiting at a runway.

A second, smaller pattern sits alongside it at several airports: an off-block stamped almost exactly a day before takeoff, giving a taxi-out of roughly 86,400 seconds plus a normal taxi.

Both patterns are visible in the scored data without the missing column: the scheduled time and the takeoff time both survive, so the gap between them is known. What is not known is whether a given flight is one of the stamped ones. So the model predicts it as a mixture. A classifier estimates the probability p that a flight's off-block was stamped at the schedule, a separate regressor predicts an ordinary taxi, and the prediction is

p × (takeoff − schedule) + (1 − p) × normal taxi

It also explains the extreme rows that had no explanation. Of the departures in 2025 recorded at over six hours, have their off-block at the schedule and more have it a day early. remain unexplained.

09 · strategies tested

Three of these eight made things worse

Every number below is a measured validation run. The ones that made things worse are worth as much as the ones that helped. Each follows from a reasonable premise, and none survives contact with the data.

The first failure is the instructive one. If a group of rows were genuinely unpredictable, the best available answer under a squared metric would be its average, so substituting the group mean should cost nothing. It costs a great deal, which establishes the opposite: the unmatched flights carry real signal, and a model does find it. With hindsight, much of that signal is the stamped schedule.

The second is smaller. Adding explicit flags for which fields are missing should, in principle, beat leaving the model to infer it from nulls. In one model over every flight it does not. The same flags do help inside the model for the unmatched group, where they separate rows within the group instead of restating what two million nulls already say.

The third is the surprising one. The airline strongly predicts whether a flight's off-block is stamped: some carriers at LIRF are stamped almost every time, others almost never. Fed to the model as a category, it still made the score worse. With seventeen thousand unmatched training flights spread over hundreds of airlines, the model memorises carriers instead of learning from them. A smoothed per-airline rate, given only to the classifier, did no better than leaving it out.

10 · a bound with a price

Where to cap a prediction

Predictions have to be bounded somewhere. Taxi-out cannot be negative, and an unbounded regressor can emit nonsense. The upper bound looks like a formality. It is a modelling decision with a measurable cost, and two hours is a plausible-sounding place to put it.

The cap belongs beyond anything the training data reaches, a full day, where it constrains nothing except a runaway.

11 · one more piece of luck

The scored months are the hard ones

The challenge ranks entries on January and July. Those happen to be two of the three most variable months of the year: winter operations at one end, peak summer congestion at the other.

This makes the validation split load-bearing rather than incidental. Holding out January and July matches the distribution the entry is scored against. Holding out a quiet spring month instead would produce a comfortable number with nothing behind it, because the tail that dominates the metric is thinner there.

What is still wrong

The model sits at on the scored months, down from for a single model, and nothing has been submitted yet. The open problems:

  • The two halves now weigh about the same. The unmatched group is down to RMSE and of the squared error. If it were predicted perfectly the overall score would be ; if every other flight were predicted perfectly it would be . For the first time, the matched flights are the larger target.
  • What is left in the unmatched group looks like noise. Its worst validation rows are two Paris departures recorded at 23 and 16 hours of taxi-out, although they took off within 35 minutes of their scheduled time. Between them they carry a fifth of all the validation squared error. Nothing in the scored columns flags them.
  • flights have a negative taxi-out, down to seconds, of them at Zurich. The two timestamps disagree there in a way that has no explanation yet.
  • Congestion barely registers. The counts of recent movements around each departure carry about one percent of the model's gain, which is odd for a problem whose physics is queueing, and the most likely place for the matched flights' remaining error.
  • The simulator has not earned its place. A separate model of the departure surface (pushback, apron, runway queue with wake separation) reproduces about a sixth of the real queueing delay and correlates weakly with it. Its output is not currently used.

The metric chose the problem, and the problem turned out to be bookkeeping.

That is the transferable part. Taxi-out is a queueing problem and the aviation literature treats it as one, but under RMSE the ordinary queueing behaviour, which a gradient-boosted tree already handles well, is not where the score was decided. It was decided on flights whose defining characteristic is a missing database record, and the productive work was not modelling why they run long. It was finding that, mostly, they do not.
