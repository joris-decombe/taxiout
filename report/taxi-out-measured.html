Taxi-Out, Measured

How long an airliner spends between leaving its stand and lifting off, why most of the error in predicting it sits in a few flights, and what the airport around each departure gives away.

A1Pushback

From stand to wheels-up

A departure's taxi-out starts when the aircraft leaves its parking position, pushed back by a tug or under its own power, and ends when its wheels leave the runway. In between it follows a taxiway to the runway, in winter perhaps stops at a de-icing pad, and waits at the holding point until the tower clears it to line up and take off.

At these ten airports the median is . The drive itself is a few minutes at 10 to 20 knots. The rest is waiting: for other aircraft on the taxiway, for a gap in arrivals when crossing a runway, and in the queue at the holding point, where departures leave one at a time with a wake-turbulence gap between them.

That queue is also why the number matters. An airliner taxiing on its engines burns fuel for every minute it stands still, so minutes of queueing multiply into tonnes of fuel and CO2 across a day at a hub. EUROCONTROL's Performance Review Commission publishes an "additional taxi-out time" indicator for this reason, and the 2026 edition of its data challenge asks for the best prediction of taxi-out itself.

BRecords

What the data keeps, and the one thing it removes

The movements come from EUROCONTROL's Airport Operator Data Flow: every airport reports every arrival and departure, with its stand, runway and times. Each departure is also linked to the Network Manager's record of its flight plan. The airport's definitions are exact:

Actual off-block time: the date and time the aircraft has vacated the parking position (pushed back or on its own power).

EUROCONTROL-SPEC-175, Airport Operator Data Flow, ODI 11

Actual take-off time: the date and time that an aircraft has taken off from the runway (wheels-up).

EUROCONTROL-SPEC-175, ODI 12

Taxi-out is not measured separately. It is take-off minus off-block: across all 2025 departures the subtraction matches the published figure to the second. So the scored file for January and July 2026 blanks the off-block time together with taxi-out. Everything else stays, and that includes arrivals with their in-block times, every take-off time, every stand and runway.

The prediction therefore runs backwards: the take-off time is known, and the task is to reconstruct when the aircraft left its stand.

CRunways

Ten airports, ten layouts

Taxi-out is first of all geography. Heathrow's two runways run either side of a crowded central terminal area, and its median taxi-out is the longest here. Schiphol's Polderbaan (36L) is several kilometres from the terminal, and departures on it take about seven minutes longer than on runway 24. Zurich chooses its runways by the clock, under noise rules and a German airspace ordinance, so the time of day largely decides the taxi route.

runwaydeparture direction, width by 2025 share

EUROCONTROL's own reference for an unimpeded taxi works at this level: the 10th percentile of taxi-out for each stand and runway pair over twelve months, valid only where at least ten flights fall at or below it. The model starts from the same reference and learns what adds to it.

DHolding point

A score that punishes the worst miss

Entries are ranked by root mean square error: square each miss in seconds, average, take the square root. A ten-minute miss costs a hundred times a one-minute miss. The same predictions, measured two ways on the January and July 2025 flights held back from training:

Sort those held-back flights from worst prediction to best and add up their squared misses. If errors were spread evenly the curve would follow the diagonal.

The worst predictions cluster in one group: about fifteen departures in a thousand whose airport record never linked to a flight plan. They have no Network Manager off-block, no callsign, no airline. Most of them taxi like anyone else. A few are recorded at hours.

SCHED = AOBTE

Records that are not measurements

At Rome Fiumicino, the recorded off-block time of of departures equals the scheduled departure time to the minute, against 1 to 6% at the other airports. For those flights the published "taxi-out" is take-off minus schedule. A flight that left four hours late shows four hours of taxi-out.

scheduled 14:25:00 · off-block recorded 14:25:00 · take-off 18:41:30 · taxi-out 15,390 s (illustrative)

Most of these records are harmless. The flag is commonest on flights the Network Manager saw leave within a few minutes of schedule, where take-off minus schedule is close to a normal taxi anyway. But on the rare delayed ones it creates the largest errors in the dataset: of Rome's departures with a flight-plan record and a recorded taxi-out over an hour are flagged rows.

The flag follows the data source more than the aircraft: some airlines have it on half their flights and others almost never, it peaks on late-evening schedules, and it is three times as common when the filed flight plan was never updated. The records look filled in from the timetable when no actual time was captured.

The scored file keeps the schedule and the take-off time, so the schedule gap is known for every 2026 departure. Whether a given record was filled in is not. The model hedges between the two readings:

prediction = p × (take-off − schedule) + (1 − p) × normal taxi

where p is a classifier's estimate that the off-block was copied from the schedule. Under a squared score this blend is the best single guess. Among flights with no flight-plan record the same pattern reaches at Rome, and a rarer one appears at several airports: an off-block logged a day before take-off, so the taxi-out reads about 24 hours plus a normal taxi.

FQueue

The airport around each departure

Blanking one column leaves the rest of the airport in view. For every 2026 departure the scored file shows which aircraft blocked in at the same stand and when, how many departures took off from the same runway in the minutes before and after, how many aircraft had pushed back without yet taking off, and which runways were in use that hour. Each of these reads the queue the departure was part of.

Tried and rejected

GDe-icing pad

January 2026 was a winter month

Aircraft de-iced on the stand wait before off-block, which does not count. Aircraft de-iced at a remote pad do it during taxi-out, which does. The data shows which airports do which: in 2025 snow, taxi-out stretches at Paris, Munich and Istanbul, while at Heathrow the delay lands before off-block instead.

The months held back for validation could not show this. January 2025 was mild at most of these airports; January 2026 was not.

Runway use moved as well. Heathrow ran easterly, taking off from 09R, far more often in 2026. Paris used 27R for departures in July 2025, apparently during works on the northern runways, and not at all in July 2026. The model learned its runway and queue effects from 2025's mix.

09R-27LH

Cleared for take-off

Each upload to the competition is scored on the real January and July 2026 flights, and only a team's best score counts. The validation score has tracked the test score only loosely: the first model did better on the test set than on validation, later ones did worse, which is what a snowier January and a different runway mix would produce.

Still ahead

  • Winter, measured on a winter. The live reading of how far other departures run over their reference is in the model, but January 2025 was too mild to show its de-icing side. A February and December 2025 holdout can.
  • Stands that opened in 2026. Munich's stands 103 to 108 and a handful of new Frankfurt positions (consistent with its Terminal 3 opening) never appear in 2025. They now borrow a reference from neighbouring stands or their runway; only the 2026 score can say what that is worth.
  • Where the validation gain went. Uploads that differ from the last one in a single airport or month measure each slice's real 2026 contribution, which validation cannot.
  • Rome. Still the airport with the largest error per flight, almost all of it from off-blocks copied from the schedule on delayed flights.
