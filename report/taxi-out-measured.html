Taxi-Out, Measured

Most of the error in predicting how long planes taxi comes from a handful of flights. Many of those were not slow planes at all. Their departure time was simply never recorded.

How long a plane spends between leaving its gate and taking off, how a competition scores predictions of it, and what turned up when we looked at where the predictions go wrong.

01 · the thing being predicted

A plane leaves its gate. Nothing happens for a while.

When a plane is ready to depart, a tug pushes it back from the gate. This moment is called pushback. The plane then drives to the runway and takes off. The time between pushback and takeoff is called taxi-out time, and at a busy European airport it averages about seventeen minutes.

Very little of that is driving. The trip from gate to runway is usually a couple of kilometres at cycling pace, five or six minutes of actual movement. The rest is waiting: for a clear path across the apron (the area around the gates), and above all in a queue at the end of the runway, where planes take off one at a time with a safety gap between each.

02 · why it matters

Engines burning fuel while going nowhere

A typical short-haul jet burns around ten kilograms of fuel a minute while taxiing. Multiply by the minutes spent standing in a queue, then by the couple of thousand departures a day at a large airport, and ground delay becomes a real share of an airport's emissions, burned without taking anyone anywhere.

Good predictions help in two ways. They show an airport when it is running beyond comfortable capacity. And they let controllers keep a plane at the gate with its engines off, instead of in a queue with them running. That second one is what saves the fuel.

This is the subject of the 2026 PRC Data Challenge, a competition run by EUROCONTROL (the organisation that coordinates European air traffic) and the OpenSky Network.

03 · how predictions are scored

What "most accurate" turns out to mean

Entries are ranked by a measure called root mean square error, or RMSE. For each flight you take how far off the prediction was, square it, average those squares over all flights, and take the square root to get back to seconds. From here on this page calls it simply the score. Lower is better.

The squaring is what matters. It means a prediction that misses by ten minutes does not count ten times as much as a one-minute miss. It counts a hundred times as much. A score built this way barely notices lots of small misses, and is dominated by a few big ones.

A score that squares its errors is not asking you to be right on average. It is asking you never to be badly wrong.

So the useful question is not how well the model does in general. It is which flights it gets badly wrong, and why.

04 · what the competition gives you

Taxi-out is a subtraction, not a measurement

Each departure in the data carries several timestamps. Two matter here: pushback and takeoff. Taxi-out is not measured on its own. It is takeoff minus pushback. Across all departures in the 2025 data, that subtraction matches the published taxi-out to the second, every time.

So the competition cannot hand out both timestamps, or anyone could subtract one from the other and win. One has to be removed, and which one decides what the task is.

Which way round the task runs

The takeoff time is there and the pushback time is not. So the prediction works backwards from the intuitive direction: you know when the plane took off, and you work out when it left the gate. You cannot see this in the practice data, where both times are present. It shows only in the scoring file, where the pushback column is empty.

05 · the first model

A respectable number that explains nothing

The model is a standard machine-learning method (gradient-boosted decision trees) that learns patterns such as "from this gate to that runway at 7am, taxi-out usually takes fourteen minutes". It only uses information that is present in the scoring file: the airport, the gate, the runway, the aircraft type, the time of day, how busy the airport was, and how long the drive from that gate takes when nothing is in the way.

That is a real improvement over guessing, and it is the point where people usually start tuning. None of that is worth doing before finding out where the error actually comes from.

06 · where the error comes from

Almost all of it is in almost none of the flights

To test the model fairly, January and July 2025 were held back and never shown to it during training. Take every flight in those two months, sort them from worst prediction to best, and add up their squared misses as you go. If the error were spread evenly, the line would follow the diagonal. It does not.

So the question is what those flights are.

07 · the first answer

The flights with no paperwork

The airport's record of each departure is linked to EUROCONTROL's flight-plan record for the same flight, which holds the callsign, the airline, the aircraft type and that second pushback time. For about fifteen departures in a thousand, the link fails. When it fails, everything from the flight plan is missing at once.

These no-record flights are the expensive ones.

The difference is at the extremes. Their slowest 1% start more than four times later, and of the departures in 2025 that took more than six hours are no-record flights.

The hardest part of this aviation problem is a database link that sometimes fails.

A missing record says nothing about the plane. It says something about the paperwork. Which raises the obvious suspicion: maybe the extreme taxi times are a paperwork problem too.

08 · the second answer

One airport writes the schedule where the pushback should be

Split the no-record flights by airport and one stands out. Rome Fiumicino has of them in 2025, under a fifteenth of the group, yet those flights account for of the group's variation.

The timestamps show why. For of Rome's no-record departures, the pushback time is identical to the scheduled departure time, to the second. At the other nine airports, that happens to of no-record flights. Those planes did not push back at exactly their scheduled second. Someone, or some system, wrote the scheduled time into the pushback field.

For those flights, the published "taxi-out" is really takeoff minus scheduled departure. If a flight left four hours late, its taxi-out reads four hours. None of that is a plane waiting at a runway. It is a delay, recorded in the wrong place.

A second, rarer pattern appears at several airports: a pushback logged almost exactly a day before takeoff, so the taxi-out reads roughly 24 hours plus a normal taxi.

The scoring file still has both the scheduled time and the takeoff time, so the gap between them is known for every flight. What cannot be known is whether a particular flight is one of the rewritten ones. So the model hedges. One part estimates the chance that a flight's pushback was rewritten with its schedule. Another part predicts an ordinary taxi. The prediction blends the two:

chance × (takeoff − schedule) + (1 − chance) × ordinary taxi

If there is a 30% chance, it takes 30% of the schedule gap and 70% of an ordinary taxi. Under a squared score, that blend is the best guess available. This also explains most of the extreme cases. Of the departures in 2025 recorded at over six hours, have their pushback equal to the schedule and more have it a day early. unexplained.

09 · what was tried

Three of these eight made things worse

Each line below is one idea, scored on the held-back months. Lower is better. The ideas that failed taught as much as the ones that worked.

Guessing the average failed badly. If the no-record flights were pure noise, the best guess for all of them would be their average, and using it should cost nothing. It costs a lot, so there is a real pattern in these flights for a model to find. Much of that pattern, it turns out, is the rewritten schedule.

Flagging blank fields helped only in the right place. Telling a single model which fields are blank should, in principle, help. It did not, because the model already copes with blanks on its own. The same flags do help inside the separate model for no-record flights, where they tell those flights apart from each other.

The airline did not help, even though it should. Some airlines at Rome have their pushback rewritten almost every time, others almost never. But with seventeen thousand no-record flights spread over hundreds of airlines, the model memorised individual airlines instead of learning from them. Giving it each airline's historical rewrite rate instead did no better.

10 · a limit with a price

Where to cap a prediction

Predictions need limits. Taxi-out cannot be negative, and a model left unchecked can occasionally produce an absurd number. The upper limit looks like a formality. It is not, and two hours sounds like a sensible place to put it.

The cap belongs beyond anything the data reaches, at a full day, where it only catches a genuine malfunction.

11 · a harder test than it looks

The scored months are hard ones

The competition scores January and July. Those are two of the three most unpredictable months of the year: winter weather at one end, peak summer traffic at the other.

That is why the model is tested on January and July 2025: the same months it will be scored on in 2026. Testing on a quiet spring month would give a flattering number, because the extreme cases that dominate the score are rarer then.

12 · where it stands

What is still wrong

The model scores on the held-back months, down from for the first model. Submitted to the competition, it scored 370.9 seconds on the real January and July 2026 flights, 139th of 200 teams; the leading team was at 221. What remains:

  • The two groups now matter about equally. The no-record flights score on their own and make up of the total squared error. If they were predicted perfectly, the overall score would be . If every other flight were predicted perfectly, it would be . For the first time, the flights with a record are the bigger target.
  • What is left among the no-record flights looks like noise. The two worst are Paris departures recorded at 23 and 16 hours of taxi-out, although they took off within 35 minutes of their scheduled time. Between them they make up a fifth of all the squared error in the test. Nothing in the scoring file warns of them.
  • flights have a negative taxi-out, as low as seconds, of them at Zurich. The plane apparently took off before it left the gate. There is no explanation yet.
  • Congestion barely registers. The model makes almost no use of how busy the airport was around each departure. That is odd for a problem that is mostly about queueing, and the likeliest place for the remaining error on ordinary flights.
  • The simulator has not earned its place. A separate program that simulates the queue (planes pushing back, driving out, and taking off one at a time with safety gaps) reproduces only about a sixth of the real queueing delay. It is not used by the model.

The scoring rule chose the problem, and the problem turned out to be bookkeeping.

That is the part that carries over to other problems. Taxi-out is a queueing problem, and the aviation research treats it as one. But under a squared score, the ordinary queueing, which the model already handles well, is not where the result was decided. It was decided on flights with a missing record. The useful work was not explaining why those flights take so long. It was finding that, mostly, they do not.
